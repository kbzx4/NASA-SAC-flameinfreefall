The finding · the self-extinction line

Somewhere between ISS air and Moon-base air, flames stop putting themselves out.

In NASA's FLEX tests with nothing added to the nitrogen, about half the burning fuel droplets in ISS air went out on their own before the fuel was used up. At the 30% oxygen planned for Moon bases, almost none did. A model fitted to FLEX tests puts the 50-50 point at oxygen, depending on the fuel: right around the 21% oxygen astronauts breathe today.

The gap

Past that line, the data thins out. Crews have lived far beyond it.

In the NASA tables loaded here, every microgravity fire test that records its surrounding air ran at % oxygen or less. Crews have breathed pure oxygen from Mercury to Apollo, about three-quarters oxygen on Skylab, and pure oxygen inside every US spacesuit today; Apollo 1 burned in pure oxygen. In the PSI tables, solid materials, the things that actually burn in a cabin, were flame-tested in orbit only up to %. NASA's inverse-flame tests (ACME CFI-G and Flame Design) did feed up to % oxygen, but out of the burner into a chamber of fuel and nitrogen, not as the surrounding air, so they are in the catalog but not on the map.

NASA's technical reports go further than its PSI tables. They add solid-material fires in orbit with the pressure recorded (the squares on the map): Saffire IV to VI burned fabric and plastic in a cargo spacecraft at up to % oxygen and psi, and the Space Shuttle's SSCE burned paper and plastic at % oxygen.

What the data shows

Gravity decided whether silicone burned.

NASA's Saffire-II experiment burned the same materials in orbit and on the ground. NASA's challenge statement says the two results could bound what happens in between. This is where that bound is tight, and where it is wide open. Then a NASA drop-tower experiment tested the idea directly.

Gravity changed the outcome for of samples.

Thin silicone did not burn in microgravity and burned completely in normal gravity. Cotton-fiberglass fabric burned completely in both. The Moon and Mars lie somewhere along each bar, and no PSI test says where.

GSI, the Gravity Sensitivity Index, is the difference between the two burn lengths divided by the sample length. 0 means they agree; 1 means the sample burned fully in one and not at all in the other. The bound is a hypothesis: it assumes burning changes steadily with gravity. *NASA's table lists this thickness as "037 mm"; it may mean 0.37 mm and is shown as listed. Source: PSI-99, Saffire-II.

From a NASA report · Moon and Mars gravity

At Moon gravity, materials kept burning at lower oxygen than on Earth or in microgravity.

NASA Glenn spun samples in a centrifuge inside a 5-second drop tower to make Moon and Mars gravity, and lowered the oxygen until the flame went out. Each bar runs from the oxygen level where the flame went out to the level where it kept burning; the dot is the middle. Lower means the material is harder to keep from burning.

The microgravity tests had a 30 cm/s airflow. Drop tests last about 5 seconds, so a flame that seemed steady might have gone out given more time. Mylar and Ultem ran at 10.2 psi, Nomex at 14.7 psi. A 2026 NASA abstract (Ferkul, Kralik and Liao) says the oxygen needed to burn reaches a minimum near Moon gravity, and NASA's FM2 experiment is to burn samples on the Moon itself. So for these materials' oxygen limits, the microgravity and Earth results did not bound Moon gravity. Source: Olson and Ferkul, ICES 2012, NTRS 20130010991, Table I (Moon values from Ferkul and Olson, 2011), transcribed by our SI coding assistant and checked against the report's printed margins.

Method

Built so you can check it.

Every number traces to NASA

Each record links to its page in NASA's Physical Science Informatics archive. Numbers transcribed from NASA technical reports name the report, table and page. Where we converted a unit, the original text sits beside the result. Where a NASA table is silent, the cell stays empty.

SI, checked by code

Code works out every finding and its ranking. A Gemini model then interprets the findings, reading only the facts code computed. Code looks up every number it wrote, a second model re-reads every sentence against the facts, and any sentence that fails either check is removed.

Honest about limits

Flamebound shows how much evidence exists near your conditions and what models fitted to NASA's droplet and gas-flame tests predict there. The models cover only the conditions tested and say nothing directly about cabin materials. It is not an operational safety system.
